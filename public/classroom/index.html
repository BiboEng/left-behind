Left Behind · Classroom guide

Evidence after the signal

A 45-minute activity built on real NASA records. Students investigate machines that stopped working on the Moon and Mars, then curate a small exhibition that keeps the evidence and its limits together.

ForGrades 6–9 · groups of 3–4
Ontario fitGrade 6 Science and Technology, Strand E: Space · Grade 9 Science (SNC1W), Strand E: Earth and Space Science
You needOne device per group, or a projector. Sound is optional.

Big question

What can a machine teach us after it stops working?

Lesson plan

  1. 0–5 min
    Hook. Show InSight's final dusty selfie on the landing page. Ask: Is this space junk? Was the mission a success? Record the first answers.
  2. 5–30 min
    Investigate. Give each group one investigation from After the Signal: Ranger 7, Spirit, InSight or Apollo 17. Groups fill in their row of the worksheet. One thing they observed, what scientists interpreted, and what the record still cannot tell us.
  3. 30–40 min
    Curate. Each group presses Keep this story, then the class builds one exhibition with a title and a question for the next reader. Print it, or share its link.
  4. 40–45 min
    Return to the hook. Did anyone change their answer? Which evidence changed it? What would you need to measure next?

Discussion prompts

  • Ranger 7 was built to crash. Can a crash be a success?
  • Spirit's soil looked bright. Why wasn't the photo enough to say "silica"?
  • InSight's audio is sped up 60 times. What does that change, and what doesn't it?
  • Apollo 17's lander became a source of vibrations. Does a direction match prove where a quake came from?

Canadian connection

Phoenix carried Canada's weather station, which detected snow falling on Mars. Curiosity carries Canada's APXS spectrometer, whose science team is led from the University of Guelph. Ask: How does a country take part in a mission it didn't launch?
